PART 2: THE RESEARCH QUESTION
================================================================================

Central Question:
  Can we predict whether a hospital patient will be readmitted within
  30 days of discharge, using only information available at discharge?

Why this matters:
  - 30-day readmissions cost the US healthcare system ~$26 billion per year
  - Medicare penalizes hospitals with high readmission rates under HRRP
    (Hospital Readmissions Reduction Program)
  - Early identification of high-risk patients allows targeted interventions:
    discharge planning, follow-up calls, post-acute care referrals
  - MIMIC-IV is one of the only publicly available real clinical datasets
    at this scale -- making this study replicable and publishable

Research framing for paper:
  "We develop a machine learning pipeline for 30-day all-cause hospital
  readmission prediction using MIMIC-IV v3.1, a large de-identified EHR
  dataset from Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center (2008-2022). We
  compare Logistic Regression, Random Forest, and XGBoost classifiers
  and evaluate model fairness across demographic subgroups."

Key design choices that distinguish this from simple tutorials:
  1. Cohort is built from raw admissions data, not a pre-packaged dataset
  2. ICD-9 and ICD-10 codes are both mapped to clinical categories (CCSR/CCS)
  3. Planned readmissions (elective, surgical same-day) are excluded from
     the positive class -- this is methodologically correct
  4. Train/test split is by patient (subject_id), not by row -- this
     prevents data leakage from patients with multiple admissions
  5. Fairness audit by race, gender, and insurance type is included

SECTION 3: Readmission Label (cells 19-28)
----------------------------------------------------------------

Purpose: Create the binary target variable readmitted_30d.
This is the most methodologically important section for the paper.

3A -- Compute Label (cell 21)
  Sort df_cohort by [subject_id, admittime].
  Use groupby + shift(-1) to get each admission's NEXT admission:
    next_admittime  = groupby(subject_id)[admittime].shift(-1)
    next_is_planned = groupby(subject_id)[is_planned].shift(-1)
  Compute:
    days_to_next = (next_admittime - dischtime).dt.days
    is_last_admission = next_admittime.isna()

  Label logic (np.select, first matching condition wins):
    Condition 1: is_last_admission == True      --> NaN  (no next admission)
    Condition 2: days_to_next <= 0              --> NaN  (data error: next
                 admission before/same day as discharge)
    Condition 3: next_is_planned == 1           --> 0    (planned return,
                 not a readmission by clinical definition)
    Condition 4: days_to_next <= 30             --> 1    (unplanned
                 readmission within 30 days)
    Default:                                    --> 0    (no readmission)

  Why np.select instead of apply():
    apply() with a row function is slow on 80k+ rows.
    np.select evaluates vectorized boolean arrays and is ~100x faster.
    The first-matching-wins semantics handles all edge cases correctly.

  Why planned returns get label=0 not NaN:
    If we dropped planned returns entirely we would bias the dataset.
    The admission IS a real next event -- we just classify it as
    "did not meet the clinical definition of readmission."
    Label=0 (not readmitted) is correct for planned returns.

  Value counts before dropping:
    0.0:  43,648
    NaN:  22,794
    1.0:  17,298

3B -- Drop Unresolvable Rows (cell 23)
  Rows dropped:
    NaN readmitted_30d: 22,794 (20,000 last admissions + 2,794 data errors)
    Negative days_to_next remaining: 0 (already captured by NaN label)
  Total dropped: 22,794

  Result: df_labeled
    Shape: 60,946 rows x 28 columns
    Unique patients: 19,234 (766 patients had ALL admissions dropped --
                     their non-last admissions all had days_to_next <= 0)
    Unique admissions: 60,946

  Why 19,234 patients instead of 20,000:
    766 patients had every non-last admission result in a data error

PART 7: KEY DESIGN DECISIONS FOR THE PAPER
================================================================================

Decision 1: Why 30-day readmission (not 7-day or 90-day)?
  - 30 days is the CMS (Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services)
    standard for the HRRP penalty program
  - Most clinical literature uses 30 days as the benchmark
  - Short enough to reflect acute care quality, long enough to capture
    delayed complications

Decision 2: Why exclude in-hospital deaths?
  - Patients who died cannot be readmitted
  - Including them would create systematic label=0 rows for the
    highest-severity patients, biasing the model incorrectly
  - Standard practice in readmission prediction literature

Decision 3: Why flag planned admissions as label=0 instead of dropping them?
  - Dropping planned admissions would remove real data points
  - A patient scheduled for an elective surgery DID return to hospital --
    but it was not a readmission in the clinical sense
  - Label=0 (no readmission) correctly reflects this: the patient was
    discharged and did not experience an unplanned readmission
  - This is the methodologically correct approach per readmission literature

Decision 4: Why require 2+ admissions?
  - A patient's first-ever admission has no previous admission history,
    and their last admission has no known subsequent admission
  - Patients with only 1 admission provide only label=NaN rows
    (last admission has no label) -- they contribute nothing
  - Requiring 2+ ensures every patient contributes at least one
    labeled row (their non-last admissions)

Decision 5: Why split by subject_id not by row?
  - One patient can appear in both train and test if split by row
  - The model would then "remember" patient characteristics from
    their training admissions when predicting their test admissions
  - This is data leakage and inflates evaluation metrics
  - Patient-level split is the correct approach for longitudinal EHR data

Decision 6: Why stratified sampling?
  - Simple random sampling of 20,000 from 98,312 could accidentally
    undersample rare age-gender combinations (e.g., very young or
    very old patients)
  - Stratified sampling preserves the demographic distribution of the
    full eligible cohort in the sample
  - This makes the sample representative and the results generalizable

Decision 7: Why use CCSR/CCS instead of raw ICD codes?
  - ICD-10 has 70,000+ codes, ICD-9 has 14,000+ codes
  - Using raw codes as features would create a massive sparse matrix
    with extreme dimensionality
  - CCSR groups ICD-10 codes into ~530 clinically meaningful categories
  - CCS groups ICD-9 codes into ~260 categories
  - This reduces dimensionality while preserving clinical signal
  - Each CCSR/CCS flag is a binary: 1 = patient had any diagnosis in
    that category during this admission

When explaining this project, use this narrative:

"I built a 30-day hospital readmission prediction system using MIMIC-IV,
a real de-identified clinical database from Beth Israel Deaconess Medical
Center. The dataset has 546,000 hospital admissions from 364,000 unique
patients, including diagnosis codes, demographics, and admission details.

My pipeline has seven stages:
  1. Raw data exploration -- understanding what the data looks like
  2. Cohort definition -- applying clinical inclusion criteria
  3. Label construction -- computing who was readmitted within 30 days
     using a clinically correct definition (excluding planned returns)
  4. EDA on the labeled dataset -- finding patterns by demographic group
  5. Feature engineering -- converting ICD codes to CCSR clinical categories
  6. Preprocessing and modeling -- training Logistic Regression, Random Forest,
     and XGBoost with patient-level train/test split to prevent data leakage
  7. Fairness audit -- checking whether the model performs equally across
     race, gender, and insurance groups

The most interesting methodological choice is how I define a readmission.
A patient returning for a scheduled surgery is NOT a readmission -- it was
planned. Only unplanned returns within 30 days count. This distinction is
clinically meaningful and affects about 56,000 admissions in the dataset.

The best model (XGBoost) achieves a ROC-AUC of 0.75, meaning it can
distinguish between patients who will and will not be readmitted 75% of
the time, significantly better than random chance (0.5)."
